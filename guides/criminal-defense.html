    Criminal Defence & Your Rights

    Arrests, police questioning, bail, charges and records — the rights that protect you and the mistakes that sink cases.

    If you are investigated, stopped or charged, the most important rights you have are the right to remain silent and the right to a lawyer. Criminal cases are decided on evidence, and many people unintentionally create evidence against themselves by talking. This guide explains how to use your rights calmly and lawfully.

    Your key rights

    • The right to remain silent beyond identifying yourself where the law requires it.
    • The right to a lawyer, and in many places to a free lawyer if you cannot afford one.
    • Protection against unreasonable searches — you may refuse consent to a search, though police may still search if they have legal authority.
    • The presumption of innocence: the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.
    • The right to know the charges and to see the evidence against you (disclosure/discovery).
    • The right to a reasonable bail decision in many systems.

    Step-by-step plan

    1. Stay calm and do not resist. Even if you believe the stop is unlawful. Challenge it later in court, not on the street.
    2. Say clearly: 'I want to remain silent and I want a lawyer'. Then stop talking about the incident, including to cellmates and on recorded jail phones.
    3. Do not consent to searches. Say 'I do not consent to a search' calmly; do not physically interfere.
    4. Contact a defence lawyer or public defender. Before any interview, plea or court appearance.
    5. Never miss a court date. Missing court can lead to a warrant and additional charges.
    6. Stay off social media about the case. Posts are routinely used as evidence.

    Documents to gather

    • Charge sheet / citation
    • Bail or release conditions
    • Court date notices
    • Names of witnesses
    • Any receipts, messages or video supporting your account

    What it may cost

    Public defenders or legal aid are available to people who qualify financially. Private defence lawyers often charge flat fees per stage (pre-charge, trial) — always get the fee agreement in writing.

    When to talk to a lawyer

    Always. Any criminal charge — even a minor one — can carry a record that affects jobs, travel and immigration status.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I have to answer police questions?

    Generally no beyond required identification. You can politely decline and ask for a lawyer.

    Should I take a plea deal?

    Only after a lawyer has reviewed the evidence and explained consequences, including immigration and employment effects.

    Can my record be cleared?

    Many places allow expungement, record suspension or pardons after a waiting period. Eligibility rules vary.

    What if I cannot afford a lawyer?

    Ask the court for a public defender or apply for legal aid as early as possible.
