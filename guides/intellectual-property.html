    Intellectual Property & Online Content

    Copyright, trademarks, patents, DMCA takedowns, fair use and protecting your brand and creative work online.

    Intellectual property (IP) is legal ownership of creations of the mind: writing, music, software, designs, inventions and brand names. For creators and businesses online, IP is often the most valuable asset — and the easiest to lose by accident.

    On this pageYour rightsStep-by-stepDocumentsCostsWhen to call a lawyerFAQ

    Your key rights

    • Copyright protects original works automatically when they are fixed in a tangible form; registration adds important enforcement benefits in some countries.
    • Trademarks protect brand names, logos and slogans used in commerce; rights grow with use and are strengthened by registration.
    • Patents protect new, useful, non-obvious inventions for a limited time, but only if you apply before public disclosure deadlines.
    • Fair use / fair dealing allows limited use of copyrighted material for purposes like criticism, commentary, news and education.
    • You can send takedown notices to platforms hosting infringing copies of your work, and you can counter-notice if your work was wrongly removed.

    Step-by-step plan

    1. Identify what you own. Content, code, logos, product names, inventions, trade secrets.
    2. Search before branding. Check national trademark databases before investing in a name.
    3. Put agreements in writing. Contractor work is not always owned by the client unless the contract assigns it.
    4. Register what matters. Trademark and copyright registration can unlock statutory damages and faster enforcement.
    5. Monitor and enforce. Use takedown notices, cease-and-desist letters and platform brand-protection programmes.

    Documents to gather

    • Original files with dates
    • Contracts and assignments
    • Registration certificates
    • Evidence of infringement (screenshots, URLs, dates)

    What it may cost

    Government filing fees are moderate; legal fees rise with searches, oppositions and litigation. Cease-and-desist letters are a low-cost first step.

    When to talk to a lawyer

    Before launching a brand, licensing your work, receiving a cease-and-desist, or when an infringement is causing real losses.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is giving credit enough to use someone's work?

    No. Attribution does not replace permission unless the licence (like some Creative Commons licences) says so.

    Can I trademark a common word?

    Only if it is distinctive for your goods or services. Descriptive or generic terms are hard or impossible to register.

    What is a DMCA takedown?

    A notice under US law asking a platform to remove infringing material; many countries have similar notice-and-takedown systems.

    Who owns work made by a freelancer?

    Often the freelancer, unless a written agreement assigns the rights.
