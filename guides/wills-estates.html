    Wills, Trusts & Estate Planning

    Wills, powers of attorney, healthcare directives, trusts and probate — protect your family with a simple plan.

    Estate planning is simply deciding in advance who receives your property, who looks after your children and who can make decisions if you cannot. Without a plan, the law decides for you through intestacy rules — and the result may be very different from what you would choose.

    Your key rights

    • Adults of sound mind can generally decide who inherits their property through a valid will.
    • You can name a guardian for minor children in your will.
    • A power of attorney lets someone you trust handle finances or health decisions if you become unable to.
    • Beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts usually override a will — keep them updated.
    • Beneficiaries generally have a right to information about the estate and to a proper accounting by the executor.

    Step-by-step plan

    1. List what you own and owe. Property, accounts, insurance, pensions, digital assets and debts.
    2. Choose your people. Executor, guardian for children, attorney for finances and for health care.
    3. Make a will that meets formal rules. Signing and witnessing rules are strict — a technical error can invalidate the will.
    4. Add powers of attorney and a healthcare directive. These work while you are alive; a will only works after death.
    5. Consider a trust. Useful for minor children, blended families, privacy or avoiding probate in some places.
    6. Review every 3–5 years. And after marriage, divorce, births, deaths, moves or major purchases.

    Documents to gather

    • List of assets and debts
    • Account and policy numbers (kept securely)
    • Existing will or trust
    • Beneficiary designation forms
    • Property deeds
    • Contact details for executor and guardians

    What it may cost

    Simple wills can be very affordable; trusts and complex estates cost more. Probate costs and timelines vary greatly by jurisdiction.

    When to talk to a lawyer

    Use a lawyer for blended families, a business, property in more than one country, a child with special needs, significant assets or any expected family dispute.

    Frequently asked questions

    What happens if I die without a will?

    Intestacy laws decide who inherits, usually spouse and children first, and a court appoints someone to manage the estate.

    Is a handwritten will valid?

    Some places accept handwritten (holographic) wills; many do not. Check local rules.

    What is probate?

    The court process that confirms a will and authorises the executor to collect assets, pay debts and distribute property.

    Do I need a trust?

    Not everyone. Trusts help with privacy, minors, incapacity planning and probate avoidance in certain systems.
