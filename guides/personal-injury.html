    Personal Injury & Accidents

    Car crashes, slip-and-falls, dog bites and workplace injuries: how fault, insurance and compensation really work.

    A personal-injury claim asks the person or business that caused your harm — usually through their insurer — to pay for what the injury cost you. Most claims never reach a courtroom; they settle through negotiation. What you do in the first days after an accident has an outsized effect on whether you are paid fairly, so this guide focuses on the decisions that matter most.

    Your key rights

    • You can seek compensation when someone else's carelessness (negligence) caused your injury — the four elements are duty, breach, causation and damages.
    • Damages usually include medical bills, future treatment, lost wages, lost earning capacity, property damage and pain and suffering.
    • You do not have to give a recorded statement to the other side's insurance company.
    • Many places use comparative fault: you can often still recover even if you were partly to blame, but your award is reduced by your share.
    • Most injury lawyers work on contingency — no fee unless you recover — so cost should not stop you from getting an opinion.

    Step-by-step plan

    1. Get medical care immediately. Even if you feel fine. Adrenaline hides injuries, and a gap between the accident and treatment is the first thing insurers use to argue you were not really hurt.
    2. Document everything at the scene. Photos of vehicles, the location, hazards, weather, your injuries; names and contacts of witnesses; the police or incident report number.
    3. Report it, but say little. Notify your own insurer as your policy requires. Stick to facts; do not guess about speed, fault or how badly you are hurt.
    4. Keep a recovery journal. Pain levels, missed work, activities you can no longer do. These notes turn 'pain and suffering' into evidence.
    5. Track every cost. Receipts, mileage to appointments, medication, help at home. Calculate lost wages with pay stubs.
    6. Do not accept the first offer. Early offers are often made before the full extent of injuries is known. Once you sign a release, the claim is usually over for good.
    7. Watch the deadline. Every jurisdiction has a limitation period (often 1–3 years for injury, sometimes much shorter for claims against a government body). Miss it and the claim is normally lost.

    Documents to gather

    • Police / incident report
    • Medical records and bills
    • Photos and video
    • Witness names and statements
    • Pay stubs or proof of income
    • Insurance policies and all letters from insurers
    • Repair estimates

    What it may cost

    Contingency fees commonly run around 25%–40% of the recovery, sometimes rising if the case goes to trial. Ask whether case costs (expert fees, filing fees, records) come out before or after the percentage — it changes what you take home.

    When to talk to a lawyer

    Talk to a lawyer when there is a hospital stay, broken bones, surgery, lasting pain, a death, a commercial vehicle, an uninsured driver, a denial of your claim, or an insurer pushing you to sign quickly.

    Frequently asked questions

    How much is my injury case worth?

    It depends on medical costs, lost income, how clear fault is, available insurance limits and the long-term impact. Our settlement estimator gives a rough range, but only a lawyer reviewing your records can value a claim.

    Should I talk to the other driver's insurer?

    You may have to confirm basic facts, but you can decline a recorded statement and refer them to your lawyer. Anything you say can be used to reduce your claim.

    What if I was partly at fault?

    In comparative-fault systems your compensation is reduced by your percentage of fault. A few places bar recovery if you are even slightly at fault, so get local advice.

    How long does a claim take?

    Simple claims can settle in a few months once treatment ends; disputed or serious cases can take one to three years.
